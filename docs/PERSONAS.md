# Different World — the three people we're building for

Three specific personas. Not "the teacher" or "the parent" — actual
people, with names, habits, and concrete frustrations. Whenever we
argue about a design decision, we should be able to say "Maria would
do this" or "David needs this" and have a real answer.

---

## Maria — the lead teacher *(PRIMARY USER)*

Lead teacher, age 38. Sunshine Preschool, 3-year-old room.

### A day in her life

Maria arrives at 6:45 AM. By 7:00 the first child walks in; by 9:00 all
18 are there. She runs the room with two assistants — one she
trusts, one who's new this month. She's been teaching for 12 years,
the last 4 at Sunshine.

Her phone lives in her apron pocket. She glances at it maybe 60 times
a day, but most glances are 5 seconds or less — she's listening for a
crash from the block corner, watching the door for who walked in,
tying someone's shoe.

The room has spotty wifi (it dies whenever someone microwaves their
lunch). The director's office has fast wifi. The playground has no
wifi at all.

She doesn't own a laptop. She has a 2-year-old iPhone and an iPad
the program provided that she rarely uses because it's too bulky.

### What's painful right now

- **Paper attendance** done twice a day by hand, then re-entered by
  the director into a state portal. Errors happen. Inspector
  questions get blamed on her room.
- **Daily sheets to parents** — the program asks her to fill out a
  printed sheet per child with checkboxes for meals, naps, diapers,
  and a "notable moment." She handwrites 18 of these every afternoon.
  By Friday she's hand-cramping.
- **Forgetting observations.** She sees Lucas comfort another child
  who's crying — beautiful moment, perfect milestone — and by the
  time she's free to write it down, it's gone.
- **Hand-off to the afternoon teacher** is a 5-minute verbal recap.
  Sometimes she forgets to mention things. The new assistant doesn't
  know to ask about specific kids.
- **One previous program** she worked at used a tablet-based app that
  pushed everything to "the cloud." She quit when they admitted they
  didn't know where the photos were stored. She doesn't trust apps
  with kid data by default.

### What she actually needs

In priority order:

1. **Attendance in under 90 seconds** — mark all present by default,
   tap to flip exceptions, done. On her phone.
2. **A way to capture a quick observation** — "Lucas comforted Mia
   when she fell — empathy" — in the 15 seconds between the moment
   and the next thing. Voice would be ideal.
3. **The afternoon teacher's hand-off** is the record of what
   happened today, not a verbal recap.
4. **The daily sheet to parents is generated, not handwritten.** Not
   her job to assemble.
5. **Confidence about privacy.** She wants to know where the photos
   go and who can see them.

### What "good" looks like to Maria

- She closes the app at 3 PM with everything logged, no paperwork
  waiting.
- She picks up an observation 2 weeks later and remembers Lucas's
  empathy moment because she captured it.
- Inspector visits don't put her on edge — the records are right.
- New assistant can read the room's day from the app and step in
  without asking 20 questions.

### How she'd describe the app to a friend

> "Honestly? It's the first one that doesn't get in my way. I just
> use my phone. Marks them present in one go. I tell it things while
> I'm doing them. The parents get a real picture of their kid's day
> and I don't have to write the same things 18 times."

## David — the program director *(SECONDARY USER)*

Director, age 52. Runs Sunshine Preschool: 4 classrooms, 65
children, 12 staff. Owns the program.

### A day in his life

David arrives at 6:30 AM, opens the building, deals with whatever
came in overnight — a parent text about pickup, a missing payment, a
sub-teacher request. By 8:00 he's at his desk dealing with billing,
licensing paperwork, ordering supplies, returning calls.

He walks the rooms midday — chats with kids and teachers, looks for
problems. After 3:00 he reviews the day's records, checks ratios,
prepares the family-facing reports if his teachers haven't.

He has a desktop in his office, an iPad for room visits, and a phone
for everything else. He's comfortable with software but has no
patience for tools that waste his time.

He hosts the state inspector every 18 months. He's currently 6 months
out from the next one and quietly dreading it.

### What's painful right now

- **Compliance binders.** Attendance is on paper; he photocopies and
  files it monthly. Incident reports are a separate paper trail.
  Medication logs are a third. When the inspector comes, he spends
  three days assembling.
- **Ratio breaches happen and he doesn't know in real time.** One
  classroom went over ratio for 20 minutes last month because a
  teacher had to take a call. He found out from a parent.
- **Teachers ask him basic questions.** "Did Mia bring lunch
  today?" "What's Lucas's allergy?" He should already have the
  answer at his desk; instead it's in someone's head or on a paper.
- **Family complaints he can't substantiate.** "My child didn't nap
  yesterday and was a mess all night." He has no record either way.
- **Billing tied to attendance** — currently a manual end-of-month
  reconciliation. Off-by-one errors are common.

### What he actually needs

In priority order:

1. **Compliance-ready records produced as a side effect** of
   teachers using the app — attendance, ratios, incident reports.
2. **Real-time visibility into ratios across all classrooms.**
3. **A child's whole file in 3 clicks** — allergies, medical,
   authorized pickup, recent notes, attendance pattern.
4. **Reports he can export** for the state inspector and for monthly
   billing reconciliation.
5. **Confidence the teachers are using it.** If 2 of 4 classrooms
   ignore the app, the data is worse than paper.

### What "good" looks like to David

- The inspector visit prep goes from 3 days to 30 minutes.
- He sees a ratio alert on his phone the moment it happens — not the
  next day from a parent.
- A family complaint becomes a 60-second lookup, not a half-day of
  back-and-forth.
- Billing is generated, he reviews and clicks send.
- He pays $X/month for this and considers it a steal.

### How he'd describe the app to a peer director

> "Look, half my job is paper-pushing for the state. This app turned
> all of that into a side effect of what the teachers do anyway. I
> can see who's where, what happened, who's owed what. Inspector
> walked through last month and I had everything queued up in a
> folder before she sat down."

## Priya — the family *(TERTIARY USER — DEFERRED PAST v1)*

Mom of a 2-year-old at Sunshine, age 31. Full-time marketing
manager.

### A day in her life

Priya drops her daughter Anaya at Sunshine at 7:30 AM, sometimes
running late and a little frazzled. She's in meetings by 8:30 in a
downtown office.

Her partner picks Anaya up at 5:15 most days. On the rare days when
neither of them can do it, her mother is on the authorized pickup
list.

Today she got a paper sheet with checkboxes when she picked Anaya
up: ate lunch, napped 12:30–2, two diapers, and a one-line note that
read "good day!" She put it in the diaper bag. She'll throw it out
tomorrow morning.

She loves Sunshine but the visibility into Anaya's days has been
… ok. The teachers are clearly attentive but she has no idea
what her kid did between drop-off and pick-up. She doesn't want to
bother them; they're busy.

### What's painful right now

- **The paper daily sheet** is a checkbox exercise. She has no
  picture of what Anaya did.
- **No photos.** Anaya turns 3 in 4 months and Priya has almost no
  pictures of her at school.
- **No reliable way to message the teachers.** Right now it's tucked
  into the diaper bag as a note, or a quick word at drop-off when
  everyone's hectic.
- **No idea how Anaya is developing.** She compares to her friends'
  kids in playgroup and worries — silently.

### What she actually needs (when we get to family-facing in v2+)

1. **A daily summary at 5 PM** — what Anaya did, ate, napped,
   anything notable. Available without logging in (a unique signed
   link or push that her phone shows).
2. **Photos, occasionally.** Even one a week would be magic.
3. **A way to message the teacher** that doesn't require her to ping
   a personal phone number.
4. **Confidence about photo privacy** — who can see Anaya's
   pictures, where they're stored.

### What "good" looks like to Priya

- She gets a daily update without doing anything.
- She has a 6-month digital album of Anaya's preschool days.
- She doesn't have to download yet another app.
- She knows what kind of day Anaya had before she walks in the door
  at 5:15.

### How she'd describe the experience to another parent

> "It's so weird — I just get a text at 5 with a summary of her day.
> A couple of photos a week. I haven't had to install anything. Her
> teacher feels closer than any previous daycare even though I see
> her less."

## Anti-personas — who we're NOT building for

Equally important: people whose needs we will explicitly deprioritize
when they conflict with Maria, David, or Priya.

- **Multi-program operators** running 5+ schools. Their need for
  org-level dashboards conflicts with our single-program shape.
  Maybe later, not v1.
- **State / regulatory analysts.** They need reports in 50 different
  state-specific formats. Out of scope; we generate clean data and
  let the director export.
- **Babysitters / nannies.** Different shape — single child, no
  classroom. Out of scope.
- **K-12 schools.** Different audience, different state regs,
  different competition. Out of scope.

---

## The decision-shortcut

When we argue a design call, ask:

> Does this serve Maria's job-to-be-done?

If yes — strong signal to build it. If no — does it serve David's?
Priya's? Or is it a feature in search of a user? The last category
gets cut.
